# Robot Sensors Explained: How Robots See, Feel and Measure

A robot cannot act sensibly if it cannot sense. A [sensor](/atlas/concept/sensor) turns some piece of the physical world (distance, light, rotation, pressure) into a number a program can use. This guide covers the five families you will meet on almost every robot, with the specific parts beginners actually buy, what they cost, and the failure each one hides.

For the definitions, the Atlas has an entry per sensor. This post is about choosing and wiring.

## 1. Distance sensors: "how far is that?"

**Ultrasonic.** The HC-SR04 sends a burst of sound above hearing and times the echo. It measures 2 cm to 4 m with accuracy down to 3 mm and costs about $5. Trigger a pin, wait for the echo pin, multiply the time by the speed of sound. It is the first sensor most people wire to an Arduino and it teaches the whole idea of ranging in ten minutes.

Where it fails: soft surfaces (a curtain, a fluffy carpet) absorb sound and return nothing; a surface at a steep angle bounces the echo away so the robot sees empty space where there is a wall; and the beam is wide, so it cannot tell you *which* of two nearby objects it heard. See [ultrasonic sensor](/atlas/concept/ultrasonic-sensor).

**Infrared (IR) ranging.** The Sharp GP2Y0A21YK is the classic: an analog voltage that falls as distance grows, over roughly 10 cm to 80 cm, about $15. It is faster and narrower than ultrasonic and fine with soft surfaces, but direct sunlight swamps it and very dark objects reflect little. See [infrared sensor](/atlas/concept/infrared-sensor).

**IR reflectance for lines.** For line following you do not want range at all, just "is the floor under me dark or light?". A reflectance array such as the Pololu QTR-HD-09A puts nine IR emitter and phototransistor pairs in a row at 4 mm pitch for about $11, so the robot can read where the line sits under it and feed that error into a [PID controller](/atlas/concept/pid-controller).

**Lidar.** A spinning laser rangefinder. The entry-level Slamtec RPLIDAR A1 covers 0.15 m to 12 m, 360 degrees, at 8,000 samples per second and 2 to 10 Hz. It is the sensor that makes [SLAM](/atlas/concept/slam) practical on a small robot: one `/scan` topic in ROS 2 and you have a floor plan. It costs more than every sensor above combined, struggles with glass and mirrors, and is the right purchase only once you want a map. See [lidar](/atlas/concept/lidar).

## 2. Vision sensors: "what is that?"

**Cameras** give the richest information of any sensor: color, shape, text, faces. The Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 is $25 for a 12-megapixel Sony IMX708 with autofocus, on a ribbon cable straight into a Pi. The catch is that an image is just millions of numbers; software has to turn them into "a cup, slightly left." Color thresholding on an Arduino-class board can follow a red ball; anything more needs a Pi or better and [computer vision](/atlas/concept/computer-vision) software.

**Depth cameras** add a distance for each pixel. The RealSense D435 ($375) uses two infrared cameras and a projector to compute stereo depth, plus a color camera, in one USB unit. That is the standard sensor on research arms, because an arm needs *what* and *where* in the same frame. See [depth camera](/atlas/concept/depth-camera).

Where vision fails: darkness, glare, lighting that changed since training, and anything that moves faster than the processing pipeline. A model that takes 300 ms per frame on a Pi is reporting where the object *was*.

## 3. Motion and orientation: "am I tilting or turning?"

An **[IMU](/atlas/concept/imu)** combines an accelerometer (which way is down, how hard am I being shoved) and a [gyroscope](/atlas/concept/gyroscope) (how fast am I rotating). The MPU-6050 is the hobby default; SparkFun's breakout is about $33, bare modules far less. Balancing robots, drones and anything that needs a heading use one.

Where it fails: drift. Integrating a gyro's rate to get an angle accumulates tiny errors every millisecond, so after a minute the "heading" is wrong by degrees. Fusing the gyro with the accelerometer (a complementary or [Kalman filter](/atlas/concept/kalman-filter)) fixes tilt; fixing yaw needs a magnetometer or an external reference such as a lidar scan.

## 4. Position: "how far has the wheel turned?"

An **[encoder](/atlas/concept/encoder)** counts pulses as a shaft rotates. Cheap gearmotors come with a magnetic encoder on the back; a few hundred counts per wheel revolution is typical. With wheel diameter known, counts become distance, and two wheels' counts become a heading estimate. That is [odometry](/atlas/concept/odometry), and every mobile robot's position estimate starts from it.

Where it fails: wheels slip. On a rug, over a bump, or during a hard turn, the wheel turns more than the robot moves, and odometry silently drifts. This is the reason robots carry a lidar or camera: something that sees the world has to correct what the wheels believe.

## 5. Touch and force: "am I pushing on something?"

A **bump switch** is a microswitch behind a bumper: the simplest sensor that exists and still fitted to robot vacuums, because a 1-cent switch never misreads a chair leg. A **[force sensor](/atlas/concept/force-sensor)** or [force-torque sensor](/atlas/concept/force-torque-sensor) in a gripper lets an arm hold an egg without crushing it and detect contact before the motors stall. **[Tactile sensors](/atlas/concept/tactile-sensor)** spread that over a fingertip.

## Why robots carry several

No sensor above is good in every condition. An ultrasonic sensor misses the curtain the IR sensor sees; the IR sensor is blinded by the window the camera handles; the camera cannot see in the dark where the lidar is fine; the lidar cannot see the glass door the bump switch finds. Combining them is [sensor fusion](/atlas/concept/sensor-fusion), and it is why a self-driving car fuses cameras, lidar and radar ([compared here](/blog/lidar-vs-radar-vs-camera-for-robots)).

## Wiring and reading them well

- **Match voltages.** The HC-SR04 and Sharp IR are 5 V parts; an ESP32 or Pi is 3.3 V on its pins. A 5 V echo into a 3.3 V pin needs a divider.
- **Filter.** Every reading is noisy. A median of five ultrasonic readings removes the occasional 0 cm glitch; an exponential moving average smooths an IR voltage.
- **Time it.** Note how long each read takes. An ultrasonic ping to 4 m and back is about 24 ms; call it in a 1 kHz loop and the loop is no longer 1 kHz.
- **Mount it right.** Ultrasonic sensors want a clear cone ahead; IR rangers want to be level; lidars want nothing from the robot's own body in the scan.

## FAQ

**What is the best sensor for a beginner?** The HC-SR04: cheap, instant results, and it teaches timing, voltage and noise in one afternoon.

**Can a robot work with just one sensor?** A line follower works with just a reflectance array. Anything that must navigate a room needs at least distance plus odometry.

**What is sensor noise?** Random error in a reading. Good code averages or filters it; good design picks a sensor whose noise is small compared with what you need to decide.
