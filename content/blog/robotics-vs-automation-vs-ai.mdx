# Robotics vs Automation vs AI: What Is the Difference?

**Short answer:** **automation** is a goal (a task happens without a person doing each step). **Robotics** is one way to reach it (a physical machine that senses and acts). **AI** is a tool (software that learns from data or reasons) that can make a robot, or anything else, more flexible. A thermostat automates without a robot or AI; a welding arm is a robot without AI; a chatbot is AI without a body; a warehouse picker is all three.

## The formal versions

The industry definition of an industrial robot comes from ISO 8373, as used by the International Federation of Robotics: an "automatically controlled, reprogrammable multipurpose manipulator, programmable in three or more axes," fixed in place or on a mobile platform, for use in automation applications. Three words carry the weight. *Automatically controlled*: it runs without a person on the controls. *Reprogrammable*: you can give it a new job without rebuilding it. *Multipurpose*: it was not built for one task only. A dishwasher is automatic but neither reprogrammable nor multipurpose, so it is an appliance, not a robot.

**Automation** has no single standards body because it is older and broader than robots: a Jacquard loom in 1804 automated weaving with punched cards. The working definition is a process that runs without a human performing each step. Software automation (a script that files invoices) and physical automation (a conveyor that sorts parcels) are both automation; only the second might involve a robot.

**AI** is the broadest and fuzziest. In practice, in 2026, it means software whose behavior was learned from data ([machine learning](/atlas/concept/machine-learning), especially [neural networks](/atlas/concept/neural-network)) or that reasons over symbols (planning, search, language models). The term **physical AI**, which chip and robot companies now use, means AI that perceives and acts in the physical world, as in robots and autonomous vehicles, rather than on a screen.

## Eight examples, sorted

| Example | Automation? | Robot? | AI? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A thermostat | Yes | No | No |
| A spreadsheet macro that emails a report | Yes | No | No |
| A welding arm repeating one path on a car line | Yes | Yes | No |
| A robot vacuum that bumps and turns at random | Yes | Yes | No |
| A spam filter | Yes | No | Yes |
| A chatbot | No (it is a tool, not a process) | No | Yes |
| A robot vacuum that builds a map with a camera | Yes | Yes | Yes (visual SLAM) |
| A warehouse picker that recognizes items in any pose | Yes | Yes | Yes |

The two robot vacuums are the instructive pair. Early models bounced off walls using bumpers and a random walk: a robot, automating a chore, with no AI. The 2015 Roomba 980 added a camera, an optical floor sensor and visual SLAM, recognizing landmarks to correct its map. Same chore, same category of machine; the camera and mapping software are where AI entered.

## How the three relate

```
Automation  = the goal      (less manual work)
Robotics    = one means     (physical machines that sense and act)
AI          = one tool      (software that learns or reasons)
```

Most automation is not robotic: it is software, conveyors, valves and timers. Most robots are not AI-driven: the IFR counted 542,000 industrial robots installed in 2024, and the great majority run fixed, programmed paths, which is exactly why they are reliable. Most AI has no body: it recommends, translates, classifies and writes on servers.

The overlap is small, growing fast, and where the interesting problems live. AI is what lets a robot cope with a world that changes: a different object each time, a person in the way, an instruction in plain English. [AI and robotics: the complete guide](/blog/ai-and-robotics-complete-guide) covers that overlap, and [AI vs traditional robot programming](/blog/ai-vs-traditional-robot-programming) covers when to use which inside one machine.

## Why the distinction matters

**If you are choosing what to learn.** Robotics needs electronics, mechanics and control; the daily work is wiring, debugging and tuning a [PID loop](/atlas/concept/pid-controller). AI needs linear algebra, probability and data; the daily work is datasets and training runs. Automation engineering can start from scripting and process thinking, and in a factory means PLCs, sensors and safety standards. The three overlap in a career but they do not start in the same place. See [career paths](/careers).

**If you are reading the news.** "AI-powered" on a product page may mean a learned vision model or may mean an `if` statement. "Robot" may mean an autonomous machine or a remote-controlled one with a person on a joystick. "Automated" may mean a robot or may mean a spreadsheet. The claim tells you which word the marketing team liked.

**If you are buying.** A company that calls a fixed-program arm an "AI robot" has sold flexibility it does not have. The buyer expects it to handle a new part; it handles the part it was programmed for.

## A three-question test for any claim

1. **What does it sense?** No sensors, no robot: it is an appliance or an automated process. Bumpers and a cliff sensor: a simple robot. A camera, lidar or microphone: now perception matters, and AI may be involved.
2. **What does it decide?** Nothing beyond a fixed sequence: automation. Choices from sensor data by written rules: a robot with classical control. Choices from a model trained on data: AI.
3. **Who wrote the decisions?** An engineer, in code you could read: traditional. A training process, in weights nobody can read: learned. The second is not better; it is different, and it needs a safety layer around it.

## FAQ

**Is a robot always intelligent?** No. The welding arm is a robot by every definition and makes no decisions at all beyond following its program.

**Is AI always a robot?** No. Almost all AI runs on servers with no body. Physical AI is the small fraction that controls machines.

**Is a self-driving car a robot?** By the sense-decide-act definition, yes: it senses with cameras, lidar and radar, decides with learned perception and classical planning, and acts through steering and brakes. By the ISO 8373 wording it is not an *industrial* robot, because it is not a manipulator; it falls under the service-robot side of the standards.

**What is "physical AI"?** The current industry term for AI that perceives, understands and acts in the physical world, from factory robots to robotaxis. It is a rebranding of the overlap in the table above, not a new technology.
