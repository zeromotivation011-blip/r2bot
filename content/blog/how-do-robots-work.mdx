# How Do Robots Work? The Sense-Think-Act Loop in Plain English

**Short answer:** a robot runs a loop, over and over, often a thousand times a second: it **senses** the world, **thinks** about what to do, then **acts**. Sensors are the eyes and ears, a computer is the brain, motors are the muscles, and the loop closing back on itself is what separates a robot from a wind-up toy. The formal version is in the Atlas under the [sense-think-act loop](/atlas/concept/sense-think-act-loop); this is the walkthrough.

## The three steps

**1. Sense.** A [sensor](/atlas/concept/sensor) turns some piece of the physical world into a number. An ultrasonic sensor reports "37 cm to the nearest thing ahead." An [encoder](/atlas/concept/encoder) reports "the left wheel has turned 1,240 counts." A camera reports twelve million color values. A [lidar](/atlas/concept/lidar) reports 8,000 distances a second around a full circle.

**2. Think.** Software decides what to do with those numbers. It can be one line ("if the wall is closer than 20 cm, turn left"), a [PID controller](/atlas/concept/pid-controller) nudging a motor toward a target speed, a [path planner](/atlas/concept/path-planning) searching a map, or a [neural network](/atlas/concept/neural-network) deciding that the blob in the image is a cup.

**3. Act.** The computer commands an [actuator](/atlas/concept/actuator): a [DC motor](/atlas/concept/dc-motor) spins a wheel, a servo turns a joint, a gripper closes. The action changes the world, the sensors measure the new world, and the loop starts again.

The loop is the point. A toy that drives forward for exactly three seconds hits the wall on a slippery floor because it never looks. A robot that senses distance every few milliseconds stops in time whatever the floor is. That ability to notice and correct is called [feedback](/atlas/concept/feedback), and it is the single idea underneath everything in robotics.

## Three robots, one loop

### A robot vacuum

A 2015-era Roomba 980 carries bumpers, cliff sensors (so it does not fall down stairs), an infrared floor-tracking sensor that works like an optical mouse, wheel encoders, a gyro, and a camera looking at the ceiling.

- **Sense:** the bumper closes on a chair leg; the floor sensor and encoders say the robot has moved 40 cm since the last update; the camera spots a distinctive corner of a picture frame it has seen before.
- **Think:** the software marks an obstacle on its map, uses the recognized landmark to correct the small drift the encoders have accumulated, and picks a path around the chair.
- **Act:** the wheel motors turn the robot left and drive on.

That camera-landmark correction is a form of [SLAM](/atlas/concept/slam) (simultaneous localization and mapping), and it is why a mapping vacuum covers a floor in neat rows while a bumper-only one wanders. The earlier Roombas ran the same three-step loop with less in the "think" step.

### A line follower

The simplest robot worth building. Five infrared reflectance sensors look at the floor; two motors drive the wheels.

- **Sense:** sensors 1 and 2 see dark, 3 to 5 see light: the line is under the left side.
- **Think:** compute an error ("the line is 1.5 sensor-widths left of center"), feed it to a PID controller, get a steering correction.
- **Act:** slow the left motor slightly, speed up the right.

This loop runs about a thousand times a second on an [Arduino](/atlas/concept/arduino), and if you slow it to ten times a second the robot weaves off the line. Loop speed is not a detail.

### An industrial arm

A Universal Robots UR5e has six joints, each with an encoder and a motor, plus a force-torque sensor in the wrist that reads up to ±50 N.

- **Sense:** six joint angles, six joint speeds, the force at the tool.
- **Think:** compare each joint to the planned trajectory; if the wrist force exceeds a limit (one of 23 configurable safety functions), stop.
- **Act:** six motor currents, updated hundreds of times a second.

Same three steps, more of them, with a safety rule that can override the plan at any point in the loop.

## How fast the loop runs

Robots run several loops at once, nested, at different speeds:

| Loop | Typical rate | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Motor control | 1,000 Hz and up | Holding a wheel at a target speed |
| Balance and stabilization | 200 to 1,000 Hz | Keeping a drone or a two-wheeled robot upright |
| Obstacle avoidance | 10 to 50 Hz | Steering around a chair from lidar data |
| Mapping and planning | 1 to 10 Hz | Updating the map, replanning a path |
| Task decisions | Seconds | "Finished the kitchen, go to the hallway" |

The fast loops need predictable timing, which is why they live on a [microcontroller](/atlas/concept/microcontroller). The slow loops need memory and compute, which is why they live on a Linux computer. Most real robots carry both: a Raspberry Pi thinking slowly on top of an Arduino reacting quickly underneath. [Choosing a board](/blog/arduino-vs-esp32-vs-raspberry-pi-for-robots) walks through that split.

## What goes wrong without each step

- **No sensing:** the three-second toy. It replays a recording and is right only while the world matches the recording.
- **No thinking:** a sensor wired straight to a motor. It reacts, but it cannot plan, remember or prioritize; it will chase the nearest wall forever.
- **No acting:** a sensor that logs the world and changes nothing. Useful, but a weather station, not a robot.
- **Slow loop:** the line follower that weaves, the drone that oscillates, the arm that overshoots. Usually the first bug a beginner meets, and usually fixed by moving the fast loop to a microcontroller.

## Feel it yourself

Build the loop with your hands before reading more theory:

1. Wire an ultrasonic sensor and one motor to an Arduino. Write a loop: read distance, stop if under 20 cm, else drive. That is the whole idea in twelve lines.
2. Put the robot on a rug, then on a tile floor. Watch it stop in time on both. Now comment out the sensor read and watch it hit the wall on the tile. You have just seen feedback.
3. Try the interactive [sense-think-act simulator](/visualizer/sense-think-act) to change the loop rate and watch what happens, or Lesson 1 of the free Spark track in the [Academy](/academy).

## FAQ

**Is a thermostat a robot?** It senses (temperature), thinks (above or below the setpoint) and acts (switches the heater). Most definitions also want a body that moves or manipulates the world and a decision beyond one threshold, so a thermostat is usually called a control system, not a robot. The loop, though, is identical.

**Do robots need AI?** No. The welding arms that make up most of the world's robots run fixed programs through the same loop. AI enters the "think" step when the world is unpredictable: recognizing an object, understanding an instruction. See [AI and robotics](/blog/ai-and-robotics-complete-guide).

**What is the best way to start learning?** Build the loop above on an Arduino, then read [robot sensors explained](/blog/robot-sensors-explained) to understand what the sensing step can and cannot tell you.
