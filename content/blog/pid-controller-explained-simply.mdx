# PID Control Explained Simply: How Robots Stay on Track

**Short answer:** a **PID controller** keeps something at a target value, such as a motor at 100 rpm or a robot on a line, by combining three signals: **P** (how far off you are now), **I** (how long you have been off), and **D** (how fast the error is changing). Details are in the [PID controller](/atlas/concept/pid-controller) entry.

## Driving a car at 60 km/h

- **P, proportional:** the further below 60 you are, the harder you press the pedal.
- **I, integral:** if you stay a little below 60 for a long time, press a bit more to remove the lingering gap.
- **D, derivative:** if you are approaching 60 fast, ease off early so you do not overshoot.

## The formula in one line

output = Kp × error + Ki × (sum of past errors) + Kd × (change in error)

Kp, Ki and Kd are three numbers you tune. This works because of [feedback](/atlas/concept/feedback): the robot measures, compares with the target, and corrects.

## What goes wrong without each part

- **Only P:** the robot settles slightly short of the target, or oscillates if P is too large.
- **Too much I:** it overshoots and swings back and forth.
- **No D:** it overshoots when it moves quickly.

## Simple tuning recipe

1. Set Ki and Kd to zero.
2. Raise Kp until the robot reacts quickly, then back off a little when it starts to wobble.
3. Add Kd to calm the wobble.
4. Add a small Ki only if a steady gap remains.

## Where you will use it

Line-following robots, balancing robots, drone stabilisation, motor speed control, and temperature control. You can try it on an [Arduino](/atlas/concept/arduino) with a motor and an [encoder](/atlas/concept/encoder).

## FAQ

**Do I need to know calculus?** Not to use it. The idea is just "present error, accumulated error, rate of error".

**Is PID the only controller?** No, but it is the most common starting point and is used everywhere.
