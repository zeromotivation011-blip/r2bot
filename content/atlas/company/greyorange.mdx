GreyOrange is a warehouse automation company. It started by building robots that carry shelves to people, and it now mainly sells the software that decides which robot should do what — including robots it did not build.

## The problem it exists to solve

In an ordinary warehouse, an order arrives and a person walks to wherever those items are stored, picks them, and walks back. Across a shift that is a lot of walking — commonly 10 to 15 kilometres — and almost none of it is the actual work. [Goods-to-person](/atlas/concept/warehouse-robotics) automation inverts it: the shelf comes to the person.

GreyOrange's **Butler** is a squat, wheeled [autonomous mobile robot](/atlas/concept/autonomous-mobile-robot) that drives under a movable rack, lifts it, and carries it to a picking station. Published figures put the heaviest racks it handles at around 1,600 kg. A **Butler PickPal** adds a six-axis arm and [machine vision](/atlas/concept/computer-vision) to do some of the picking itself, at a reported 500–600 picks per hour for items up to about 4 kg.

If that sounds like [Amazon Robotics](/atlas/company/amazon-robotics), it is the same idea. The difference is who can buy it: Amazon's robots are for Amazon. GreyOrange sells to everyone else.

## Who built it, and where it is from

Samay Kohli and Akash Gupta founded GreyOrange in 2011, both BITS Pilani graduates who had met through university robotics competitions. The company's early engineering and manufacturing were in India, and its first large deployments were with Indian e-commerce — which is why it is often described as an Indian robotics company.

That description has aged. GreyOrange registered in Singapore, opened a US headquarters and manufacturing site in Atlanta in 2018, and runs R&D across India, the US and Singapore with offices in Japan and Germany. Today its announcements and its leadership are Atlanta-based. It is a useful reminder that a robotics company's nationality is usually a question with several answers.

The 2018 Series C was reported at the time as the largest single round ever raised by an industrial robotics company. Mitsubishi, Flipkart and Blume Ventures are among the investors.

## The pivot that matters

The more interesting story is what GreyOrange became. A warehouse that buys robots from one vendor is locked to that vendor; a warehouse that buys from several ends up with fleets that cannot see each other. GreyOrange's answer is **GreyMatter**, an orchestration platform: software that sits above the robots, decides which machine does which task, and treats hardware as interchangeable.

That turned a robot manufacturer into a software company whose robots are one option among many. Its Certified Ranger Network admits third-party machines to the platform. Recent developments follow the same line — a Google Cloud collaboration on DeepNav for autonomous robot navigation (2025), a partnership with Zebra for overhead RFID inventory sensing (January 2026), and GreyMatter Foundry (April 2026), a simulator for planning a warehouse layout before buying anything.

## Why it is worth knowing about

Most people learning robotics picture a humanoid or a robot arm. The machines that actually changed an industry are these: unglamorous wheeled boxes that lift shelves. And the lesson of GreyOrange's last five years is one the whole field is learning — that past a certain fleet size, the hard problem stops being the robot and becomes deciding what all of them should do next.
