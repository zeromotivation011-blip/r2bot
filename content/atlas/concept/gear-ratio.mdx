A gear ratio is the number of turns a gearbox's input makes for **one turn of its output**. A motor geared at 30:1 turns 30 times for every turn of the wheel or joint it drives, so the output is 30 times slower and, apart from friction losses, 30 times stronger. It is the single number that tells you what a gearbox does to a motor's speed and torque.

This page is about the number: how to calculate it and how to use it. For the hardware that produces it (simple, compound and planetary arrangements, idlers, worm and strain-wave drives) see [gear train](/atlas/concept/gear-train).

## How to calculate a gear ratio

For two meshing gears, the ratio is the number of teeth on the driven gear divided by the number on the driving gear:

```text
N = driven teeth / driving teeth = ω_in / ω_out
```

A 12-tooth motor gear driving a 36-tooth gear gives N = 36 / 12 = 3, written **3:1**. For several stages in series, multiply: a 3:1 stage followed by a 4:1 stage gives 12:1. Gears that sit between two others on their own shafts (idlers) cancel out of the calculation.

### Reading the notation

"3:1" usually means a **reduction**: the input turns 3 times per output turn. Some sources write the same reduction as 1:3, so read the context. The MIT Cheetah paper, for example, writes its leg actuator's planetary gear train as "1:5.8" in one place and "5.8:1 gear reduction" in another, for the same gearbox. A ratio below 1 (N = 0.5: the output turns twice as fast as the input) is an **overdrive**, rare in robot joints.

## What the ratio does to speed and torque

```text
output speed  = input speed / N
output torque = input torque × N × η
```

Here η (eta) is the gearbox efficiency. With no losses, power in equals power out (torque × speed), so torque rises by exactly as much as speed falls. Real gearboxes lose some of it as heat, and they lose more with every stage. The maxon GP 32 C planetary gearhead datasheet is a good example of typical figures:

| Stages | Reductions offered | Max. efficiency |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3.7:1 to 5.8:1 | 80% |
| 2 | 14:1 to 33:1 | 75% |
| 3 | 51:1 to 190:1 | 70% |
| 4 | 246:1 to 1,093:1 | 60% |
| 5 | 1,181:1 to 6,285:1 | 50% |

So a high ratio buys a lot of torque, but each extra stage takes a bigger slice of it.

## Worked example: gearing a robot's wheels

A two-wheeled robot weighs 10 kg and has wheels 0.1 m in diameter. Each wheel motor runs at 3,000 rpm with a 30:1 two-stage gearbox (take 75% efficiency).

**Top speed.**

```text
wheel speed = 3,000 rpm / 30        = 100 rpm
robot speed = 100/60 rev/s × π × 0.1 m ≈ 0.52 m/s
```

**Torque to climb a 5° ramp** (ignoring rolling resistance and acceleration):

```text
force along the slope = m · g · sin 5° = 10 × 9.81 × 0.087 ≈ 8.5 N
per wheel (two wheels)               ≈ 4.3 N
torque per wheel = 4.3 N × 0.05 m    ≈ 0.21 N·m
motor torque     = 0.21 / (30 × 0.75) ≈ 0.0095 N·m
```

Choose a 60:1 gearbox instead and the motor works about half as hard (a little more if the extra stage lowers the efficiency), but the robot's top speed halves to about 0.26 m/s. That is the gear-ratio decision in one line: **speed against force**, with efficiency taken off the top.

## The hidden effect: inertia grows with N²

A gearbox also changes how heavy the motor feels from the output. The motor rotor's inertia appears at the output multiplied by the **square** of the ratio:

```text
J_reflected = J_rotor × N²
```

The MIT Cheetah's leg actuator, for example, has a rotor inertia of 3.0279 × 10⁻⁴ kg·m², which its 5.8:1 gear train turns into 0.0102 kg·m² at the leg. Behind a 51:1 gearbox the same rotor would reflect about 0.79 kg·m², roughly 77 times more. That is why a joint with a very high ratio is hard to push by hand and hits obstacles harder, and why legged and collaborative robots often choose low ratios on purpose (see [backdrivability](/atlas/concept/backdrivability)).

## How to choose a ratio

1. **Start from the task.** Work out the torque the joint or wheel needs (load, slope, acceleration) and the top speed it needs.
2. **Divide by the motor.** The ratio must be at least required torque ÷ (motor's continuous torque × η), and at most motor's top speed ÷ required output speed.
3. **Pick inside that window**, leaning low if the robot touches people or the ground hard (backdrivability, reflected inertia) and high if it must hold heavy loads precisely.
4. **Check the gearbox, not only the motor.** Gearboxes have their own torque and input-speed limits; the GP 32 C, for instance, is rated for 1 N·m continuous in its one-stage version and 6 N·m from three stages up.
5. **Check the backlash** if position accuracy matters: more stages usually means more play (see [backlash](/atlas/concept/backlash)).

## Common questions

### What is a gear ratio?

A gear ratio is the number of times the input of a gear train turns for one turn of the output. For two gears, it is the driven gear's tooth count divided by the driving gear's. A ratio of 30:1 makes the output 30 times slower and, before friction losses, delivers 30 times the torque.

### How do you calculate gear ratio?

Divide the number of teeth on the driven (output) gear by the number on the driving (input) gear. For multiple stages, calculate each stage and multiply the results. You can also measure it: count input revolutions for one output revolution.

### Does a higher gear ratio mean more torque?

Yes. Output torque is input torque times the ratio times the efficiency, so a higher reduction ratio gives more torque and less speed. The gain is reduced by friction (more stages mean lower efficiency) and comes with more reflected inertia and often more backlash.

### What does a 100:1 gear ratio mean?

The input shaft turns 100 times for each turn of the output. Output speed is 1/100 of the motor speed, and output torque is up to 100 times the motor torque, less the gearbox's losses: a gearbox that is 60% efficient delivers about 60 times the motor torque.
