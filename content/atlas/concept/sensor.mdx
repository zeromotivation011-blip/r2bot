A **sensor** is a device that detects something physical — light, sound, distance, heat, motion, force — and turns it into an electrical signal a computer can read. In a robot, sensors are the only link between the machine and reality: without them a robot can only replay stored instructions, with no way of noticing that the world has moved.

The textbook definition is wider than robotics: a sensor is a device that receives and responds to a signal or stimulus, which makes a thermometer a sensor too. What makes one a *robot* sensor is that software reads it many times a second and acts on it immediately.

## What a sensor actually does

Every sensor performs the same trick: it finds a physical effect that changes predictably with the thing you want to measure, then measures that effect instead. A thermistor's resistance changes as it warms, so measuring resistance measures temperature. An ultrasonic rangefinder times an echo. A camera measures brightness at millions of points and lets [software](/atlas/concept/computer-vision) infer the rest.

That indirection is why no sensor is perfect, and why three numbers on a datasheet matter far more than the product name:

- **Sensitivity** — how much the output changes for a given change in the thing being measured.
- **Resolution** — the smallest change the sensor can detect at all.
- **Accuracy** — how close the reading is to the truth. It can be considerably worse than resolution, because of offset, non-linearity, drift and hysteresis.

The HC-SR04, the cheap ultrasonic module on most beginner robots, is specified for a ranging distance of 2 cm to 4 m, a measuring angle of 15°, and accuracy reaching about 3 mm. Those figures say what it is for — stopping a small robot before it hits a wall — and what it cannot do: map a warehouse, or see anything narrower than its 15° cone.

## The main sensor families

| Family | What it measures | Typical robot use |
|---|---|---|
| Rangefinders — [ultrasonic](/atlas/concept/ultrasonic-sensor), infrared, [lidar](/atlas/concept/lidar) | Distance to the nearest surface | Obstacle avoidance, mapping, navigation |
| Cameras — colour, [depth](/atlas/concept/depth-camera), thermal | Light arriving at a grid of pixels | Object recognition, inspection, visual navigation |
| Inertial — [IMU](/atlas/concept/imu) | Rotation rate and acceleration of the robot's own body | Balancing, flight stabilisation, heading estimates |
| Position — [encoders](/atlas/concept/encoder), potentiometers | How far a joint or wheel has turned | Closing the loop on every motor |
| Force and touch — load cells, [force-torque sensors](/atlas/concept/force-torque-sensor) | Push, pull and twist at a point of contact | Delicate gripping, assembly, safe contact with people |
| Environment — temperature, humidity, gas, light | Conditions around the robot | Monitoring, agriculture, safety shutdowns |

## Looking outward and looking inward

Sensors that look *outward* — cameras, lidar, rangefinders — report on a world the robot does not control. Sensors that look *inward* — encoders, IMUs, motor current, joint torque — report what the machine itself is doing. A useful robot needs both.

Inward sensing is fast and dependable but accumulates error: encoders that say you have travelled three metres cannot know the wheels were spinning on a wet floor, which is why [odometry](/atlas/concept/odometry) drifts. Outward sensing corrects that drift, but is slower, noisier and easily fooled — glass defeats most rangefinders, and a camera is only as good as the light.

## Why one sensor is never enough

Because every sensor fails in its own specific way, serious robots carry several that overlap and combine their readings through [sensor fusion](/atlas/concept/sensor-fusion). The point is not redundancy for its own sake; it is that the failure modes do not coincide. The fused estimate is what the control software actually believes — and [actuators](/atlas/concept/actuator) act on it. That loop, running many times a second, is what separates a [robot](/atlas/concept/robot) from a machine following a script.

## Common questions

### Is a sensor the same thing as a transducer?

Not quite. A transducer converts one form of energy into another in either direction — a loudspeaker is a transducer too. A sensor is the input case: it converts a physical quantity into a signal to be read. In everyday robotics talk the two words are used interchangeably.

### How many sensors does a robot need?

As many as it has things it cannot afford to get wrong. A line follower needs two. A self-balancing robot needs an IMU plus wheel encoders. At the far end, NASA's Perseverance rover carries 23 cameras alone, on top of its other instruments.

### Can better software fix a bad sensor?

Partly. Filtering and fusion reduce noise and cancel some drift — much of what a navigation stack does. What software cannot do is invent information the sensor never captured: if the resolution is too coarse, or the sensor cannot see the thing at all, no amount of processing recovers it. Choose the sensor for the job first, then filter.
