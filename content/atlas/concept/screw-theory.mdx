**Screw theory** is a way of describing motion and force in which every movement of a solid object is a single **screw motion**: a turn about one axis combined with a slide along that same axis. Every force on the object, including any twisting effect, is described the same way. Robotics uses it because it lets one compact set of numbers describe what a robot arm's joints do, how fast the hand moves, and what forces it can push with.

## Start here: the screw in your hand

Drive a screw into wood. It turns and moves forward at the same time, along one line. Now look at two everyday extremes:

- A **door on its hinge** turns but never slides along the hinge line. That is a screw with no forward motion.
- A **drawer** slides but never turns. That is a screw that is all forward motion.

The surprising part is that *every* way a rigid object can move, however twisted the path looks, can be described as one screw motion about one line in space. This result is known as the **Chasles–Mozzi theorem** ([Lynch & Park, ch. 3](https://hades.mech.northwestern.edu/index.php/Modern_Robotics)). So instead of tracking a jumble of rotations and translations, you only need the screw's axis, how much it turned, and how far it slid.

## How it works: twists, wrenches and pitch

Screw theory gives two matching objects, each a list of six numbers ([Lynch & Park, ch. 3](https://hades.mech.northwestern.edu/index.php/Modern_Robotics)):

| Object | Describes | Six numbers made of | Everyday picture |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Twist** | How a body is moving right now | angular velocity (3) + linear velocity (3) | a spinning, advancing screw |
| **Wrench** | The force acting on a body | moment, i.e. twisting effect (3) + force (3) | turning a screwdriver while pushing |

The **pitch** of a screw is how far it slides along its axis for each unit of rotation. Robot joints fall neatly into this picture:

| Joint | What it does | Pitch |
|---|---|---|
| Revolute (hinge) | rotates only | zero |
| Prismatic (slider) | translates only | infinite (pure translation) |
| Helical (lead screw) | rotates and translates together | a fixed, finite value |

Twists and wrenches fit together: the dot product of a twist and a wrench (multiply matching entries and add them up) gives **power**, the rate at which the force does work on the moving body ([Lynch & Park, ch. 3](https://hades.mech.northwestern.edu/index.php/Modern_Robotics)). This is why one framework can handle both "where is the hand going?" and "how hard can it push?".

## Where you'll see it

- **Robot arm kinematics.** In the *product of exponentials* formulation, [forward kinematics](/atlas/concept/forward-kinematics) is written by taking the hand's pose with every joint at zero, then applying each joint's screw motion in turn. You need only the joint axes and that one "home" pose, not a coordinate frame on every link ([Lynch & Park, ch. 4](https://hades.mech.northwestern.edu/index.php/Modern_Robotics)).
- **The Jacobian.** The [Jacobian](/atlas/concept/jacobian), which turns joint speeds into hand speed, has the joints' screw axes (in the current pose) as its columns ([Lynch & Park, ch. 5](https://hades.mech.northwestern.edu/index.php/Modern_Robotics)).
- **Grasping and contact.** When fingers hold an object, each contact can push only in certain directions; describing those forces as wrenches lets you check whether a grasp can resist any push ([Murray, Li & Sastry, ch. 5](https://pages.github.berkeley.edu/EECS-106/fa24-site/assets/MLS.pdf)).
- **Teaching.** The textbook *Modern Robotics* (Lynch & Park, 2017), whose preprint is free to read online, builds its whole treatment of kinematics on screws.

## Going deeper

For readers comfortable with linear algebra: the set of rigid-body poses forms the group **SE(3)**, and twists live in its Lie algebra **se(3)**. A screw motion of angle θ about axis S is the matrix exponential e^[S]θ, so a chain of joints is a product of exponentials: T(θ) = e^[S₁]θ₁ · e^[S₂]θ₂ ⋯ e^[Sₙ]θₙ · M, where M is the home pose ([Lynch & Park, ch. 3–4](https://hades.mech.northwestern.edu/index.php/Modern_Robotics); [Murray, Li & Sastry, ch. 2–3](https://pages.github.berkeley.edu/EECS-106/fa24-site/assets/MLS.pdf)).

Compared with [Denavit-Hartenberg parameters](/atlas/concept/denavit-hartenberg), this avoids the rules for placing a frame on every link, and the joint axes can be read straight off a drawing of the robot. The cost is more mathematics up front.

The theory itself is much older than robots: Robert Stawell Ball's *A Treatise on the Theory of Screws* (Cambridge, 1900) set out screws, twists and wrenches for mechanics long before anyone applied them to robot arms ([Ball, 1900](https://archive.org/download/theoryscrews00ballrich/theoryscrews00ballrich.pdf)).

## Why it matters

Screw theory turns robot motion into geometry you can see: every joint is a line in space, and every motion and force is measured along those lines. That makes kinematics, velocity and force one subject instead of three, which is why modern robotics courses teach it.

## Common questions

### What is screw theory in simple words?

It is the idea that any movement of a solid object can be described as one screw-like motion, turning about a line while sliding along it, and that any force on the object can be described the same way. Robot arms are chains of hinges and sliders, so this gives one language for the whole arm.

### What is the difference between a twist and a wrench?

A twist describes motion: how fast the body is turning and moving. A wrench describes force: how hard it is being pushed and twisted. Both are six numbers built around a screw axis, and their dot product gives power.

### Is screw theory better than Denavit-Hartenberg?

Neither is wrong; both give the same robot pose. Screw theory (product of exponentials) needs no frame on each link and its joint axes are easy to read from a drawing. Denavit-Hartenberg uses only four numbers per joint, but each link needs a frame placed by strict rules ([Lynch & Park, appendix C](https://hades.mech.northwestern.edu/index.php/Modern_Robotics)).
