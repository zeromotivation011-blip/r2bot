A depth sensor, usually built as a **depth camera**, measures how far away every point in its view is. Instead of a colour for each pixel, it gives a distance, so one frame is enough for a robot to know where the floor, the walls, the table and the cup on the table are in 3D.

![How the three main kinds of depth sensor measure distance. Stereo: two cameras a known distance apart see the same point at slightly different positions, and the shift (disparity) gives the depth, z = f times B divided by d. Structured light: a projector casts a known pattern of infrared dots, and one camera offset from it sees the pattern shift on near and far surfaces. Time-of-flight: the camera sends out modulated infrared light and measures how long it takes to come back, from the phase shift of the returning wave. All three produce the same output: a depth image where each pixel is a distance, which becomes a point cloud](/images/atlas/diagrams/depth-sensor.svg)

## Start here: a photo of distances

Picture a black-and-white photo of a room where the shade of each pixel means distance instead of brightness: the wall behind is dark, the chair in front is light. That image is a **depth map** (or depth image), and a depth sensor produces one many times a second.

The name varies. "Depth sensor", "depth camera", "range camera" and "3D camera" usually mean the same device. When the depth image comes with an aligned colour image from the same unit, the pair is called **RGB-D** (red, green, blue plus depth). Many depth cameras sold for robotics, such as the RealSense D435 and the OAK-D, are RGB-D cameras.

Why robots care: a colour camera tells a robot *what* is there, but working out *where* it is from one flat image is hard. A depth camera answers "where" directly, at video rate, from a box small enough to bolt onto a small robot. The TurtleBot 4, a common robot for learning ROS 2, carries one (an OAK-D stereo camera) next to its spinning [lidar](/atlas/concept/lidar).

## The three ways to measure depth at every pixel

Almost every depth camera uses one of three principles. Two of them are triangulation, like your two eyes; the third times the light.

| | Stereo (passive or active) | Structured light | Time-of-flight (ToF) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Principle | Two cameras; depth from the shift between their images | One camera sees how a known projected pattern shifts | Time (or phase delay) for emitted light to return |
| Needs texture on surfaces? | Yes (an IR projector adds texture indoors) | No, the pattern is the texture | No |
| Bright sunlight | Works; RealSense says its D415 and D435 tend to do even better in bright light | Projected pattern is easily drowned out | Ambient light lowers signal-to-noise; designs vary |
| Typical weak spot | Featureless walls; error grows with distance squared | Outdoor use; like stereo, error grows with distance squared | Multipath in corners, maximum unambiguous range |
| Examples | Intel RealSense D435, Luxonis OAK-D | First Kinect (Xbox 360); Face ID's dot pattern | Azure Kinect DK, Kinect for Xbox One |

Each of these has its own Atlas entry in more depth: [stereo vision](/atlas/concept/stereo-vision), [structured light](/atlas/concept/structured-light) and [time-of-flight sensor](/atlas/concept/time-of-flight-sensor). Here is the core idea of each.

### 1. Stereo: two eyes and a little geometry

Two cameras sit a fixed distance apart; that distance is the **baseline**, B. A point close to the cameras appears at noticeably different horizontal positions in the left and right images; a far point appears at almost the same position. That shift, measured in pixels, is the **disparity**, d. Intel's RealSense paper gives the relationship:

```text
z = f · B / d

z  depth (same units as B)
f  focal length, in pixels
B  baseline between the two cameras
d  disparity, in pixels
```

The hard part is finding the same point in both images, which needs visible texture. On a blank white wall every patch looks alike, so the match is ambiguous. **Active stereo** fixes this by adding an infrared projector that sprays a random dot pattern onto the scene; the paper describes its job as generating "texture which makes image matching unambiguous". The cameras do not need to know the pattern, which is the difference from structured light. The projector lets an active stereo camera work in the dark, and because the cameras can also use natural texture lit by the sun, it keeps working outdoors.

### 2. Structured light: a known pattern, one camera

A projector casts a pattern the system already knows, typically infrared dots or stripes, and a camera set a little to one side photographs it. On a near surface the pattern lands in a different place in the image than on a far one, and the shift gives depth by the same triangulation as stereo. The first Kinect, for the Xbox 360, worked this way. Apple's Face ID uses the same idea at arm's length: the TrueDepth camera "captures accurate face data by projecting and analyzing thousands of invisible dots" to build a depth map of your face.

Texas Instruments' introduction to depth cameras notes the catch: structured light is "sensitive to optical interference from the environment". Sunlight is the usual culprit: it washes out the projected pattern, which is why structured-light sensors are mainly indoor devices. RealSense's tuning guide makes the general point that "most depth cameras degrade dramatically in sunlight", and it is the main reason stereo, which can use sunlight, is preferred outdoors.

### 3. Time-of-flight: timing the light

A ToF camera lights the whole scene with infrared and measures, at every pixel, how long the light took to return. Distance is half the round trip at the speed of light, c. Light covers a metre and back in about 6.7 nanoseconds, so most ToF cameras do not time a pulse directly. They modulate the light at a frequency f and measure the **phase shift** φ of the returning wave. TI gives:

```text
d     = c · φ / (4π · f)     distance from phase shift
d_amb = c / (2 · f)          maximum unambiguous distance
```

At f = 20 MHz the unambiguous range is about 7.5 m; at 100 MHz it is about 1.5 m. A higher frequency gives better precision but wraps around sooner, so advanced ToF cameras combine several frequencies to extend the range. Microsoft's Azure Kinect DK is a phase-based camera: it "implements the Amplitude Modulated Continuous Wave (AMCW) Time-of-Flight (ToF) principle" with a 1-megapixel sensor. Others time light at the photon level: Apple says its iPad Pro LiDAR Scanner "operates at the photon level at nano-second speeds" and "measures the distance to surrounding objects up to 5 meters away", indoors and outdoors.

## How a robot uses a depth image

### From pixels to 3D points

A depth image becomes useful once each pixel is turned back into a 3D point. With the camera's calibration (focal lengths fx, fy and image centre cx, cy, from the camera's `camera_info`), the pinhole camera model gives:

```text
X = (u − cx) · Z / fx
Y = (v − cy) · Z / fy
Z = depth at pixel (u, v)
```

This is exactly the arithmetic ROS 2's `depth_image_proc` package runs for every pixel. Do it for the whole image and you have a [point cloud](/atlas/concept/point-cloud): the same kind of 3D data a lidar gives, but dense and in front of the camera only.

**Worked example.** A 848 × 480 depth image, with fx = fy = 424 px, cx = 424, cy = 240. The pixel at (u, v) = (624, 240) reads 2.0 m. Then X = (624 − 424) × 2.0 / 424 ≈ 0.94 m and Y = 0: the point is 2 m ahead and about 0.94 m to the right of the camera's centre line.

### What robots do with it

- **Obstacle avoidance and navigation.** Mobile robots add depth points to their [costmap](/atlas/concept/costmap) so they see things a flat 2D lidar misses, such as a table top above the scan plane.
- **Grasping and bin picking.** An arm needs to know the 3D shape and pose of an object before it can plan where to put its fingers.
- **Mapping.** Depth frames stitched together with the camera's motion build a 3D map of a room, as in RGB-D [SLAM](/atlas/concept/slam).
- **People and gestures.** Depth makes it easier to separate a person from the background and estimate their pose.

## How accurate is a depth camera?

For stereo the answer has a clean formula. If the disparity can be matched to within a small error Δd, the depth error is:

```text
Δz ≈ z² · Δd / (f · B)
```

The z² is the important part: double the distance and the error roughly quadruples. RealSense's tuning guide puts it plainly: "The depth error scales as the square of the distance away."

**Worked example: a RealSense D435.** The D435 has a 50 mm baseline. RealSense's own guide uses an 848-pixel-wide image with a 90° horizontal field of view (so f ≈ 424 px) and a subpixel matching error of 0.08 px for a well-textured target. Plugging those in:

| Distance | Disparity | Theoretical depth error (RMS) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 m | 42.4 px | ≈ 0.9 mm |
| 1 m | 21.2 px | ≈ 3.8 mm |
| 2 m | 10.6 px | ≈ 15 mm |
| 3 m | 7.1 px | ≈ 34 mm |
| 4 m | 5.3 px | ≈ 60 mm |

These are best-case numbers on a textured surface; real scenes are noisier. They show why depth cameras are best at short range, why a wider baseline helps at long range, and why RealSense recommends 848 × 480 on the D435: lower resolution means a smaller f in pixels and more error.

Time-of-flight error behaves differently. It depends mainly on how much light returns (TI's noise equation has signal amplitude and ambient light in it), so dark or distant surfaces are noisier, and the modulation frequency sets the trade-off between precision and range. Microsoft quotes the Azure Kinect at a random error of 17 mm or less and a systematic error under 11 mm plus 0.1% of distance, within stated test conditions.

## Where depth sensors fail

Depth cameras mark pixels they cannot measure as invalid (0 or NaN), but some errors, such as multipath and edge pixels, can still come through as wrong distances. Knowing the causes helps you mount and choose one.

- **Too close.** Triangulation needs a minimum distance. RealSense's guide gives the D435's minimum at roughly 17–20 cm at 848 × 480, and notes it shrinks at lower resolution.
- **Shiny, black or transparent surfaces.** Glass lets light through, mirrors send it elsewhere, black objects send little back. Microsoft lists "Low IR signal" and "Saturated IR signal" among the reasons the Azure Kinect invalidates a pixel.
- **Corners and edges (multipath).** Light that bounces off two surfaces before returning gives a wrong distance. Azure Kinect pixels "can also be invalidated if they received signals from more than one object in the scene". Pixels on object edges mix foreground and background.
- **Featureless surfaces for passive stereo.** A white wall with no projector gives nothing to match.
- **Sunlight for structured light.** The projected pattern is lost in ambient infrared.

## Depth images in ROS 2

[ROS 2](/atlas/concept/ros2) has a convention for depth, set by REP 118. The canonical form is a `sensor_msgs/Image` "encoded as 32-bit float", where "each pixel is a depth (along the camera Z axis) in meters". Drivers may also publish the older raw form: 16-bit unsigned integers in millimetres, where "the value 0 denotes an invalid depth". Note that depth is measured along the camera's Z axis, not along the ray to the point.

The `depth_image_proc` package does the standard conversions. Its components include:

| Component | What it does |
|---|---|
| `ConvertMetricNode` | Raw uint16 millimetres to float metres |
| `PointCloudXyzNode` | Depth image + `camera_info` to an XYZ point cloud on `points` |
| `PointCloudXyzrgbNode` | Registered depth + colour image to a coloured XYZRGB cloud |
| `RegisterNode` | Reprojects depth into another camera's frame, such as the colour camera |

A minimal run looks like this; replace the topic name with your camera driver's:

```bash
ros2 run depth_image_proc point_cloud_xyz_node --ros-args \
  -r image_rect:=/my_camera/depth/image_rect
# camera_info is read from the same namespace as the image (/my_camera/depth/camera_info)
# the cloud appears on /points; add a PointCloud2 display in RViz to see it
```

## Depth camera or lidar?

Both give 3D geometry, and many robots carry both. A typical lidar sweeps a laser around the robot and measures far and outdoors (rain, fog and direct sun still cost it range), but sees the world as a set of scan lines. A depth camera sees a dense rectangle in front of it at video rate, cheaply and close up, but its stereo error grows quickly with range and some types struggle in sunlight. A common pairing on indoor robots is a 2D lidar for mapping and localisation plus a depth camera for obstacles above and below the scan plane. TurtleBot 4 carries both, although its default Nav2 setup builds its costmaps from the lidar alone. For a full side-by-side, see [lidar vs radar vs camera for robots](/blog/lidar-vs-radar-vs-camera-for-robots).

## Choosing one for a robot

- **Indoor mobile robot or arm at 0.3–3 m:** active stereo (RealSense D400, OAK-D Pro) is a common choice: the projector lets it work in the dark, and outdoors it falls back on natural texture instead of failing.
- **Outdoors:** stereo, or a sensor whose datasheet states outdoor use; check the ambient light rating rather than assume.
- **Close-range, high-detail scanning indoors:** structured light gives the densest, most accurate surfaces on untextured objects (TI rates its depth accuracy highest of the three); ToF also needs no texture but is noisier at edges and in corners.
- **Check the minimum range** against where the object will be. A wrist camera that is 15 cm from the grasp needs a short minimum distance.
- **Check the driver.** A ROS 2 driver that publishes REP 118 depth plus `camera_info` saves days.

## Where you'll see it

Depth cameras sit on research and teaching robots such as TurtleBot 4, on the wrists and heads of manipulation robots, and on warehouse and service robots that have to avoid people and objects. Outside robotics, the same principles unlock your phone (Face ID's projected dots) and measure rooms for augmented reality (the iPad Pro's LiDAR Scanner).

## Why it matters

Depth is what turns a robot's picture of the world into a model it can act on. A gripper cannot close on a colour; it closes on a position. A depth sensor is the cheapest way to get dense 3D geometry at short range, and understanding its three principles tells you in advance where it will be accurate, where it will lie, and which one to buy for the job.

## Common questions

### What is a depth sensor?

A depth sensor is a device that measures the distance to every point in its view and outputs it as an image, where each pixel holds a distance rather than a colour. Most are depth cameras that work by stereo vision, structured light or time-of-flight, and robots convert their output into a 3D point cloud.

### Is a depth camera the same as a depth sensor?

In practice, yes. "Depth camera" is the most common form of depth sensor: a camera-sized device that outputs a full depth image. When it also outputs an aligned colour image, it is called an RGB-D camera. Single-point distance sensors (ultrasonic, single-beam ToF) measure depth too but only in one direction.

### Do depth cameras work outdoors?

It depends on the type. Stereo cameras can use sunlight as their light source, and RealSense reports its D415 and D435 tend to perform even better in bright light. Structured-light cameras are easily washed out by sunlight. Time-of-flight cameras lose signal-to-noise in strong ambient light, and how much varies by design.

### Why does my depth camera show holes in the image?

Those pixels are marked invalid because the sensor could not measure them reliably: the surface is too close, shiny, black or transparent, light bounced between two surfaces (multipath), or a passive stereo camera found no texture to match. In ROS 2 the raw format marks them as 0 and the float format as NaN (±Inf can mean out of range, per REP 117).
