An **LQR controller** (short for **linear quadratic regulator**) is a feedback controller that works out its own gains. You describe the robot with a model, then state two preferences: how much you dislike each kind of **error** (the **Q** matrix) and how much you dislike using **effort** such as motor torque or thrust (the **R** matrix). LQR returns the one set of gains that best balances the two, and the robot applies them continuously: *control = −K × state*.

## Start here: balancing a broom

Balance a broom upright on your palm. Two things matter at once:

- **Error**: how far the broom is leaning, and how fast it is falling.
- **Effort**: how hard and fast you have to move your hand to catch it.

React too gently and the broom falls. React too violently and you waste energy and overshoot. [PID controller](/atlas/concept/pid-controller) gains are usually tuned by hand or with rules of thumb, one loop at a time. LQR asks a different question: *how much does each error cost you, and how much does each unit of effort cost you?* Write those costs down as numbers, and the gains follow from a calculation instead of guesswork.

That is why LQR is the classic controller for "balancing" problems: Tedrake's MIT course notes apply it to the double integrator, the acrobot, the cart-pole and a balancing Segway ([Underactuated Robotics, ch. 8](https://underactuated.mit.edu/lqr.html)).

## How it works

LQR needs three ingredients ([Tedrake, ch. 8](https://underactuated.mit.edu/lqr.html); [MathWorks `lqr`](https://www.mathworks.com/help/control/ref/lti.lqr.html)):

1. **A linear model** of the robot, written in [state-space form](/atlas/concept/state-space-control): ẋ = Ax + Bu. Here *x* is the state (angles, positions, velocities) and *u* is the input (torques, thrust).
2. **A cost** that adds up error and effort over all future time: J = ∫ (xᵀQx + uᵀRu) dt. The term xᵀQx is the price of being away from the target; uᵀRu is the price of the effort used to get back.
3. **The weights**: Q must be positive semi-definite and R positive definite (in practice, usually diagonal matrices of non-negative and positive numbers).

Solving the **algebraic Riccati equation**, SA + AᵀS − SBR⁻¹BᵀS + Q = 0, gives a matrix S, and from it the gain **K = R⁻¹BᵀS**. The controller is then simply **u = −Kx**: multiply the measured state by K, and that is the command. The expensive part (solving the Riccati equation) happens once, offline; running the controller is one matrix multiplication per time step.

A solution exists when two conditions hold: the system is *stabilizable* (the inputs can influence every unstable part of the motion), and Q does not leave any unstable or marginally stable motion completely unpenalised (formally, (Q, A) has no unobservable mode on the imaginary axis) ([MathWorks `lqr`, Limitations](https://www.mathworks.com/help/control/ref/lti.lqr.html)). With Q = 0, for example, LQR has no reason to stabilise anything.

## What Q and R mean on a drone

A quadcopter is a good example because it has many coupled states. One published outdoor flight study models a quadcopter with **12 states** and **4 inputs**, linearised around hover ([Paredes et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14231)):

| Part of the problem | What it contains | What its weight means |
|---|---|---|
| **State x** (12 numbers) | position x, y, z · velocities · roll, pitch, yaw angles · body angular rates | — |
| **Q** (one weight per state) | big weight on position → hold position tightly; big weight on angles → keep the drone level | "How bad is this error?" |
| **Input u** (4 numbers) | total thrust and the three body torques (later mapped to the four motor commands) | — |
| **R** (one weight per input) | big weight on thrust or torque → smoother, gentler flying, less battery and motor stress | "How expensive is this effort?" |

So, in the case of a drone, **Q defines which flight errors matter most** (drifting from the setpoint, tilting, spinning) and **R defines how much the controller is allowed to work the motors** to fix them. Raising Q makes the drone snappier and more precise; raising R makes it calmer and less aggressive. Only the *ratio* matters: multiplying Q and R by the same number gives the same gains.

### Choosing the first Q and R: Bryson's rule

A common starting point is **Bryson's rule**: set each diagonal entry of Q to 1 divided by the square of the largest error you can accept in that state, and each entry of R to 1 divided by the square of the largest input you can accept. MathWorks notes that this usually gives a reasonable first design but is "often just the starting point of a trial-and-error iterative design procedure" ([MathWorks `lqr`](https://www.mathworks.com/help/control/ref/lti.lqr.html)). Åström and Murray credit the rule to Bryson and Ho ([Feedback Systems, ch. 6](https://www.cds.caltech.edu/~murray/books/AM08/pdf/am08-statefbk_28Sep12.pdf)).

## Try it: the gains change with Q and R

The simplest system with a position and a velocity is the *double integrator*: a block on a frictionless track pushed by a force (ẋ₁ = x₂, ẋ₂ = u). This Python snippet computes its LQR gain with SciPy:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_continuous_are

A = np.array([[0, 1], [0, 0]])   # position, velocity
B = np.array([[0], [1]])         # force changes velocity
Q = np.diag([1.0, 1.0])          # price of position and velocity error
R = np.array([[1.0]])            # price of force

S = solve_continuous_are(A, B, Q, R)
K = np.linalg.solve(R, B.T @ S)  # K = R^-1 B^T S
print(K)                          # [[1.  1.73205081]]  i.e. K = [1, √3]
```

Change the weights and watch the gains move (values computed with the code above):

| Weights | Gain K (position, velocity) | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Q = diag(1, 1), R = 1 | 1.00, 1.73 | Baseline |
| Q = diag(10, 1), R = 1 | 3.16, 2.71 | Position error is now expensive → stiffer, faster correction |
| Q = diag(1, 1), R = 10 | 0.32, 0.86 | Force is now expensive → softer, slower correction |

## LQR vs PID

| | [PID](/atlas/concept/pid-controller) | LQR |
|---|---|---|
| Needs a model? | No | Yes, a linear state-space model |
| Inputs and outputs | Best for one input, one output per loop | Handles many coupled inputs and states at once |
| How you tune it | Three gains per loop, by hand or rules of thumb | Choose Q and R; gains are computed |
| Needs every state measured? | No, only the error signal | Yes, the full state (or an estimate of it) |
| Typical use | Motor speed, joint position, temperature | Balancing, hovering, attitude control |

Neither is universally better. LQR is only as good as its model and its weights; the quadcopter study above flew PD, PID, LQR, LQR with integrators and explicit MPC controllers on the same airframe precisely to compare them in practice ([Paredes et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14231)).

## Limits, and what comes next

- **It is linear.** Real robots are nonlinear, so LQR is designed around one operating point (upright, hovering). Tedrake describes it as "a local approximation of the optimal control solution for the nonlinear system": excellent near that point, not guaranteed far from it ([ch. 8](https://underactuated.mit.edu/lqr.html)).
- **It needs the full state.** If some states are not measured (velocities often are not), pair LQR with a [state observer](/atlas/concept/state-observer) or a [Kalman filter](/atlas/concept/kalman-filter). LQR plus a Kalman filter is called **LQG** (linear quadratic Gaussian) control.
- **It ignores limits.** Basic LQR does not know that a motor saturates or a joint has an end stop. [Model predictive control](/atlas/concept/model-predictive-control) keeps the same "cost of error plus cost of effort" idea but re-solves it every step with constraints.

## Going deeper

For readers comfortable with linear algebra, Tedrake's chapter covers the extensions used on real robots ([Underactuated Robotics, ch. 8](https://underactuated.mit.edu/lqr.html)):

- **Finite-horizon and time-varying LQR.** Instead of one constant K, the Riccati equation is integrated backwards in time to give a gain K(t). This is how a robot follows a planned trajectory (a flip, a jump, a swing-up) rather than holding one pose.
- **Discrete-time LQR.** Digital controllers run at a fixed rate, so the discrete Riccati equation is solved instead; its infinite-horizon solution is the fixed point of the discrete recursion.
- **Cross terms.** The general cost adds 2xᵀNu, giving K = R⁻¹(BᵀS + Nᵀ) ([MathWorks `lqr`](https://www.mathworks.com/help/control/ref/lti.lqr.html)).
- **Pole placement vs LQR.** [Pole placement](/atlas/concept/pole-placement) picks where the closed-loop poles go directly; LQR lets them land wherever the optimal trade-off puts them, which scales better to many inputs.

## Why it matters

LQR is the step from tuning single loops by hand to designing a controller for the whole robot at once. Its central idea, *write down what you care about as a cost and let mathematics find the controller*, runs through model predictive control, trajectory optimisation and much of modern robot control.

## Common questions

### What is an LQR controller in simple words?

It is a controller that calculates the best feedback gains for you. You give it a model of the robot and two sets of weights, one saying how much each error matters (Q) and one saying how costly each kind of effort is (R). It returns gains that balance fast correction against wasted effort.

### In LQR, what do the Q and R matrices define for a drone?

Q sets how strongly the drone should fight each error: drifting from its position, tilting, or spinning. R sets how costly it is to use thrust and torque. A larger Q gives tighter, faster flight; a larger R gives smoother flight with less motor effort. Bryson's rule (1 over the square of the largest acceptable error or input) is a common starting point.

### Is LQR better than PID?

Not always. LQR handles many coupled states at once and computes its gains from a model, which makes it a strong choice for balancing and hovering. PID needs no model and is simpler to run on one loop. In practice LQR is limited by how good the model is, and it needs the full state measured or estimated.
