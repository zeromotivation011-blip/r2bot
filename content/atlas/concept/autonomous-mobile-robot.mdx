# Autonomous Mobile Robot (AMR)

An autonomous mobile robot, or AMR, is a robot that drives itself around a building or a street, working out its own route as it goes — with nobody steering it and nothing laid into the floor for it to follow. It senses the space around it, keeps track of where it is, picks a path, and picks a new one when a person or a dropped pallet gets in the way.

## What makes it "autonomous"

The comparison is with the AGV, the automated guided vehicle, which has moved material around factories for decades. An AGV is described as a vehicle that "follows along marked long lines or wires on the floor" — a buried wire whose magnetic field a sensor picks up, magnetic tape or magnets set into the floor, ultraviolet-reactive stripes, or reflective markers on walls read by a laser scanner. The route lives in the building.

An AMR carries the route instead, so changing where it goes is a software change rather than a construction job, and it can leave its usual line to get around an obstacle and then rejoin it. AMRs are distinguished from AGVs on exactly this point: they do not depend on extra infrastructure such as magnetic strips or visual markers.

| | AMR | AGV |
|---|---|---|
| Follows | A map it carries | A guide fitted into the building |
| Guidance | Onboard sensors and planning | Wire, tape, magnets, floor stripes, reflective markers |
| Meets an obstacle | Plans a way around it | Stops until the path clears |
| Changing the route | Edit the map | Re-lay the floor |
| Suits | Spaces that change | Fixed, high-volume routes |

Neither is simply better: a route that never changes and runs all day suits an AGV, which is cheaper and has less to go wrong.

## How it knows where it is

An AMR does three jobs at once. It **senses**, usually with a [LiDAR](/atlas/concept/lidar) scanner for distances, depth cameras for what is immediately ahead, an inertial sensor for tilt and turn, and wheel encoders feeding [odometry](/atlas/concept/odometry). It **localises**: a first pass of [SLAM](/atlas/concept/slam) builds the map, and after that the robot matches what it sees against that map to work out where it is standing. And it **plans** — a global [planner](/atlas/concept/path-planning) finds a route across the map while a local controller turns the next few metres into motor commands.

The open-source reference implementation is [Nav2](/atlas/concept/nav2), which builds an environmental model from sensor and semantic data, routes a path through it dynamically, and computes feasible motor commands while avoiding obstacles. It supports "nearly any class of robot kinematics and dynamics."

Sizes vary widely. One mainstream indoor unit, the MiR250, is specified at a 250 kg payload, a 2.0 m/s top speed with maximum payload on a flat surface, and an 800 by 580 mm footprint.

## The fleet layer

One AMR is a navigation problem. Fifty in the same aisles is a traffic problem, and that is different software. Fleet management sits above the robots and decides which machine takes which job, who waits at a junction, and when a robot peels off to charge. It also has to talk to the building itself: a robot that cannot call a lift is stuck on one floor.

At scale, traffic becomes the main problem. Amazon's answer, announced with its one-millionth robot in July 2025, is DeepFleet: a generative AI model acting as a traffic management system for the fleet, cutting congestion and routing robots more efficiently, which Amazon says will improve the fleet’s travel time by 10%.

## Where they are actually deployed

[Warehouses and distribution centres](/atlas/concept/warehouse-robotics) are the big one, and the jobs are more varied than carrying boxes: Amazon's Hercules lifts up to 1,250 pounds of inventory, Pegasus handles individual packages on a conveyor deck, and Proteus is the company's first fully autonomous mobile robot — free to work in the open floor alongside people rather than inside a fence. Beyond logistics, AMRs run linens and medication between hospital floors, shuttle parts between factory cells, and deliver on pavements outdoors.

That spread into shared human space is why a standard exists: ANSI/RIA R15.08-1-2020, the first American national safety standard for industrial mobile robots, sets technical requirements for designing them so people working nearby stay safe. It applies to industrial settings.

## Common questions

### Does an AMR need Wi-Fi to work?

Navigation runs onboard, so a robot that loses the network can usually finish the move it is on. The network carries the layer above: job assignment, junction traffic, lift and door requests. Patchy coverage shows up as robots idling for instructions, not as robots driving into walls.

### Can an AMR replace an AGV in any building?

No. AMRs earn their keep where layouts change, task mixes vary, or robots share floor space with people. For one fixed, heavy, high-volume route an AGV is often cheaper, and plenty of sites run both.
