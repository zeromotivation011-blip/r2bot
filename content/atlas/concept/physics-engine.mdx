A **physics engine** is software that calculates how objects move, collide and push on each other, one small slice of time after another. Video games use them, usually as ready-made middleware, to simulate motion in real time ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_engine)). Robotics uses them so a robot can be tested, or taught by trial and error, in a virtual world before it touches real hardware.

This page is about the engines robots are simulated with. When you run a robot in [Gazebo](/atlas/concept/gazebo-simulator), the physics comes from DART by default ([Gazebo](https://gazebosim.org/api/sim/9/physics.html)); in Webots it comes from ODE ([Webots](https://cyberbotics.com/doc/reference/worldinfo)); and in [Isaac Sim](/atlas/concept/isaac-sim) the default backend is NVIDIA PhysX ([Isaac Sim](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/latest/physics/index.html)). [MuJoCo](/atlas/concept/mujoco) and [PyBullet](/atlas/concept/pybullet) are physics engines you can call directly from Python.

![The loop a physics engine repeats every time step: read the state, add forces, detect collisions, resolve contact and friction forces, integrate to get new velocities and positions, then advance time by one step](/images/atlas/diagrams/physics-engine.svg)

## Start here: a flip-book of the world

A physics engine does not solve the whole motion at once. It chops time into tiny steps and, at each step, asks four questions:

1. **What forces act right now?** Gravity, motor torques, springs, damping.
2. **What is touching what?** A foot on the floor, a gripper finger on a cup.
3. **How hard do the touching things push and rub on each other?** Enough to stop them passing through one another, with friction resisting sliding.
4. **So where is everything a moment later?** New velocities, then new positions.

PyBullet's guide sums up a step as "collision detection, constraint solving and integration" ([PyBullet](https://docs.google.com/document/d/10sXEhzFRSnvFcl3XxNGhnD4N2SedqwdAvK3dsihxVUA/edit)). The length of each step, the **time step** (Δt), matters most. MuJoCo's documentation calls it "the single most important parameter affecting the speed-accuracy trade-off" and notes that "smaller values result in better accuracy and stability" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/XMLreference.html#option)). Here are the defaults, as each project documents them:

| Engine or simulator | Default time step | Steps per simulated second |
|---|---|---|
| MuJoCo (`timestep`) | 0.002 s ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/XMLreference.html#option)) | 500 |
| PyBullet (`setTimeStep`) | 1/240 s ([PyBullet](https://docs.google.com/document/d/10sXEhzFRSnvFcl3XxNGhnD4N2SedqwdAvK3dsihxVUA/edit)) | 240 |
| SDFormat, Gazebo's world format (`max_step_size`) | 0.001 s ([SDFormat](https://github.com/gazebosim/sdformat/blob/main/sdf/1.12/physics.sdf)) | 1,000 |
| DART (`World`) | 1 ms ([DART](https://github.com/dartsim/dart)) | 1,000 |
| Webots (`basicTimeStep`) | 32 ms ([Webots](https://cyberbotics.com/doc/reference/worldinfo)) | 31.25 |

Webots warns that a larger step speeds the simulation up but "will decrease the accuracy and the stability, especially for physics computations and collision detection" ([Webots](https://cyberbotics.com/doc/reference/worldinfo)).

## How it works

### The equation underneath

Every step comes down to Newton's second law written for a whole robot at once. MuJoCo's documentation gives it as **M v̇ + c = τ + Jᵀf** ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)). In words:

- **M** is the robot's inertia (mass) matrix; **v̇** is the acceleration the engine wants.
- **c** collects gravity plus Coriolis and centrifugal effects; **τ** is applied force from motors and springs.
- **Jᵀf** is the contact and constraint force **f**, mapped into the joints by the Jacobian **J**.

Once **f** is known, the acceleration follows directly: v̇ = M⁻¹(τ + Jᵀf − c). MuJoCo computes **c** with the Recursive Newton-Euler algorithm and **M** with the Composite Rigid-Body algorithm, and is blunt about where the difficulty lies: "The computation of the constraint force is the hard part" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)). See [equations of motion](/atlas/concept/equations-of-motion) and [dynamics](/atlas/concept/dynamics) for where this equation comes from.

### Joint coordinates versus free bodies

There are two ways to describe a robot arm to a physics engine.

- **Maximal (Cartesian) coordinates.** Every link is a free body with six degrees of freedom, and each joint is a constraint that removes the motions it forbids. MuJoCo describes this as the approach of gaming engines, "where joints remove DOFs instead of adding them", and says it can cause "inaccuracies and instabilities when elaborate kinematic structures are involved" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/overview.html)).
- **Generalized, joint or reduced coordinates.** The state is the joint angles (plus the base's pose for a robot that is not bolted down). Joints cannot drift apart because they are never separate in the first place. PhysX says its reduced-coordinate articulations have "zero joint error by design" and can handle larger mass ratios between jointed bodies, and that their cost is "generally proportional to the number of degrees of freedom, rather than the number of links" ([PhysX](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.6.1/docs/Articulations.html)).

DART computes motion "with Featherstone's Articulated Body Algorithm" ([DART](https://github.com/dartsim/dart)), and PyBullet loads URDF joints the same way by default, using the Articulated Body Algorithm "for linear time forward dynamics" ([PyBullet](https://docs.google.com/document/d/10sXEhzFRSnvFcl3XxNGhnD4N2SedqwdAvK3dsihxVUA/edit)). Linear time means doubling the number of joints roughly doubles the work. For loose objects with no joints, PyBullet's maximal-coordinate option "can improve performance a lot".

### Finding what touches what

With n collision shapes there are n(n − 1)/2 pairs that could be touching ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)): 4,950 pairs for 100 shapes. Checking each pair exactly is too slow, so engines filter in stages. MuJoCo uses a **broad phase** (a sweep-and-prune pass that cheaply discards bodies that are far apart), then a mid-phase tree of axis-aligned bounding boxes, and only then the exact **narrow phase**, whose method depends on the two shape types. The output is a list of active contacts ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)). For collision checks used in motion planning rather than simulation, see [collision checking](/atlas/concept/collision-checking).

### Contact and friction: the hard part

When a foot meets the floor, real materials squash a little and push back. Engines approximate that in quite different ways, which fall into four main families:

| Approach | The idea | Who uses it, and what the documentation says |
|---|---|---|
| **Penalty (spring-damper)** | Let bodies overlap slightly and push them apart with a stiff spring and damper | MuJoCo says these models are "soft but are plagued by instabilities" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)) and need "very small time-steps" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/overview.html)) |
| **Complementarity (LCP/NCP)** | Hard contact: a contact either pushes or separates, never both, solved as a linear or nonlinear complementarity problem | MuJoCo calls the LCP family "the de facto standard" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)); DART uses "LCP-based contact with Dantzig and PGS solvers" ([DART](https://github.com/dartsim/dart)); PyBullet uses "a unified LCP constraint solver" ([PyBullet](https://docs.google.com/document/d/10sXEhzFRSnvFcl3XxNGhnD4N2SedqwdAvK3dsihxVUA/edit)) |
| **Convex soft contact** | Drop strict complementarity, allow a little softness, and solve a convex optimization with a unique answer | MuJoCo ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)) |
| **Hydroelastic** | Give objects an internal pressure field and compute a whole contact patch, not single points | Drake ([Drake](https://drake.mit.edu/doxygen_cxx/group__hydroelastic__user__guide.html)) |

Why is it so hard? MuJoCo's documentation gives three reasons ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)). Frictional LCPs "correspond to NP-hard optimization problems", which "has given rise to an industry of approximate solvers". The Coulomb friction model "is not well-defined in continuous time" (Painlevé's paradox), which has made discrete-time, "velocity-stepping schemes very popular". And the hard-contact assumption is never quite true: "all physical materials allow some deformation".

How the main engines handle it:

- **MuJoCo** treats softness as a feature: an object pushed sideways on a soft surface should "move up a bit as it starts sliding", which MuJoCo argues makes soft contact more realistic than strict hard contact. The cost is that force and separating velocity "can be simultaneously positive", effects it calls "numerically small yet undesirable" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)).
- **Drake** lets bodies "penetrate slightly, as if the rigid body had a slightly deformable layer". Its hydroelastic model "attempts to approximate the patch and pressure distribution" instead of the "single point contact or a set of point contacts" common in robotics, and by default falls back to point contact in cases hydroelastic contact cannot handle ([Drake](https://drake.mit.edu/doxygen_cxx/group__hydroelastic__user__guide.html)).
- **ODE**, as used in Webots, softens hard constraints with two parameters: ERP, the proportion of contact error fixed in the next step, and CFM, where "the softness will increase as CFM increases" ([Webots](https://cyberbotics.com/doc/reference/worldinfo)).
- **PhysX** recommends its Temporal Gauss-Seidel (TGS) solver, which "subdivides the time step into multiple equally-sized substeps", over the older Projected Gauss-Seidel (PGS) ([PhysX](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.6.1/docs/RigidBodyDynamics.html)).

Friction gets approximated too. In MuJoCo you choose between a pyramidal friction cone, the default, and an elliptic one; the documentation says "elliptic cones are a better model of the physical reality, but pyramidal cones sometimes make the solver faster and more robust" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/XMLreference.html#option)). See [friction model](/atlas/concept/friction-model) and [contact mechanics](/atlas/concept/contact-mechanics) for the physics being approximated.

### Integrators, and a worked example you can check

The integrator turns accelerations into new velocities and positions. MuJoCo's documentation sets out the two simplest ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)):

- **Explicit Euler:** v ← v + Δt·a and x ← x + Δt·v, using the *old* velocity.
- **Semi-implicit Euler:** v ← v + Δt·a first, then x ← x + Δt·v with the *new* velocity.

**A falling ball.** Drop a ball from rest with g = 9.81 m/s² and Δt = 0.1 s. Both methods get the velocity right (0.981 m/s after one step, 1.962 after two), but they disagree about distance:

| Time | Exact (½gt²) | Explicit Euler | Semi-implicit Euler |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 s | 0.04905 m | 0 m | 0.0981 m |
| 0.2 s | 0.1962 m | 0.0981 m | 0.2943 m |
| 0.3 s | 0.44145 m | 0.2943 m | 0.5886 m |
| 1.0 s | 4.905 m | 4.4145 m | 5.3955 m |

After one second both are off by 0.4905 m, in opposite directions. Cut the step to 0.01 s and the error falls to 0.04905 m; at MuJoCo's default of 0.002 s it is 0.00981 m, about a centimetre. For these first-order methods, ten times smaller steps give ten times smaller error.

**A spring.** Now take a 1 kg mass on a 100 N/m spring, pulled 1 m and released, stepped at Δt = 0.01 s. The true motion swings between −1 m and +1 m forever. With explicit Euler, every step multiplies the energy by exactly 1 + (Δt·ω)² = 1.01, where ω = 10 rad/s, so after 10 seconds (1,000 steps) the energy has grown by 1.01¹⁰⁰⁰ and the amplitude of the swing by its square root: about **145 times** too large. With semi-implicit Euler, every swing over the same 10 seconds peaks between 1.0000 m and 1.0013 m. A stiff contact spring behaves just like this, which is why stability matters as much as accuracy.

This is why MuJoCo does not implement explicit Euler at all: semi-implicit Euler is "strictly better, and standard in physics simulation" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)). It is also called symplectic Euler ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-implicit_Euler_method)). MuJoCo's default integrator is semi-implicit Euler; the alternatives include fourth-order Runge-Kutta and implicit-in-velocity variants ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/XMLreference.html#option)), which are particularly effective "where instabilities are caused by velocity-dependent forces" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)).

## Where you'll see it

- **Robot simulators.** Gazebo, Webots and Isaac Sim each wrap a physics engine with robot models, sensors and rendering. Robot models come in as URDF, SDF or MJCF files; PyBullet loads all three ([PyBullet](https://docs.google.com/document/d/10sXEhzFRSnvFcl3XxNGhnD4N2SedqwdAvK3dsihxVUA/edit)). See [URDF](/atlas/concept/urdf).
- **Training robots with reinforcement learning.** A [reinforcement learning](/atlas/concept/reinforcement-learning) policy learns by trial and error, so it practises in simulation, where a fall breaks nothing and, as MuJoCo notes, most robotic systems can be simulated "a lot faster than real-time" ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/XMLreference.html#option)). Isaac Lab, built for this, combines "GPU parallel physics", rendering and domain randomization tools, and has been applied to whole-body control and "contact-rich and dexterous manipulation" ([Isaac Lab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04831)).
- **Design, control and verification.** Drake is "a collection of tools for analyzing the dynamics of our robots and building control systems for them" ([Drake](https://drake.mit.edu/)).
- **Games and visual effects.** Bullet is built for "VR, games, visual effects, robotics, machine learning" ([Bullet](https://github.com/bulletphysics/bullet3)); ODE is "used in many computer games" ([ODE](https://www.ode.org/)).

## Limits

- **Every contact model is an approximation.** Each family above trades realism, speed or stability against the others. Gazebo's physics layer is built on the premise "that there is not a single physics engine that is universally best for all simulation contexts" ([gz-physics](https://github.com/gazebosim/gz-physics)), so the same scene can behave differently in different engines.
- **Speed and accuracy pull against each other.** To run in real time, MuJoCo notes, the time step "must be larger than the CPU time per step", and models with many floating objects, and so many contacts, are more demanding ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/XMLreference.html#option)).
- **The numbers you feed it are estimates.** Friction coefficients, masses, joint damping and motor behaviour are measured or guessed, never exact. Behaviours learned in simulation "are often specific to the characteristics of the simulator", and "due to modeling error, strategies that are successful in simulation may not transfer to their real world counterparts" ([Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06537)). This is the [sim-to-real](/atlas/concept/sim-to-real) gap.
- **Rigid is an idealisation.** Robot links are usually modelled as perfectly rigid. Some engines go further: MuJoCo natively models rods, cloth and 3D soft bodies ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/overview.html)), and PhysX on the GPU supports FEM soft bodies and particles ([PhysX](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.6.1/docs/GPURigidBodies.html)).

## Going deeper

- **GPU-parallel simulation.** Reinforcement learning needs a great deal of practice, so engines now run many copies of a robot side by side on a GPU. PhysX runs its whole rigid-body feature set, articulations included, on CUDA GPUs through the same API as on the CPU ([PhysX](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.6.1/docs/GPURigidBodies.html)). MuJoCo XLA (MJX) runs MuJoCo through JAX; its JAX implementation runs on NVIDIA and AMD GPUs, Apple Silicon and Google Cloud TPUs, and its Warp implementation is optimized for NVIDIA GPUs ([MJX](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mjx.html)).
- **Differentiable physics.** If the engine can return gradients of the next state with respect to the current one, you can optimize a controller with gradient descent instead of trial and error. MJX's JAX implementation supports automatic differentiation; MJX-Warp does not ([MJX](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mjx.html)). Newton lists differentiability among its design goals ([Newton](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton)).
- **Narrowing the gap.** Rather than chasing a perfect engine, many teams randomize what they cannot measure. Peng et al. trained a robot-arm pushing policy with randomized simulator dynamics and report "a similar level of performance when deployed on a real robot" ([Peng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06537)); Tobin et al. randomized rendering instead ([Tobin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06907)). See [domain randomization](/atlas/concept/domain-randomization).
- **Inverse dynamics with contact.** Given a recorded motion, which forces caused it? With hard contact that question has no answer: if you push against a wall without moving, the force "cannot be recovered from the kinematics". MuJoCo's soft model has a unique inverse, so it can ([MuJoCo](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/latest/computation/index.html)).

## Why it matters

The physics engine decides what a simulated robot can teach you. Its time step, contact model and coordinates determine whether a grasp that holds in simulation holds on the bench, and whether a walking policy trained in simulation survives its first real floor. Knowing the stages of a step, the main contact models and the engine inside your simulator tells you which results to trust, which settings to tune, and where to expect the [sim-to-real](/atlas/concept/sim-to-real) gap.

## Common questions

### What is a physics engine in simple terms?

It is the part of a simulation that makes things obey physics. At every tiny time step it works out the forces on each object, checks what is touching what, computes the contact and friction forces, and moves everything forward a little. Games use one to make objects fall and bounce; robot simulators use one so a virtual robot moves like a real one.

### Which physics engine is best for robot simulation?

No single engine is best for everything, which is why Gazebo lets you swap engines. MuJoCo was built for robotics and machine-learning research, PhysX is Isaac Sim's default and runs on GPUs for Isaac Lab, DART is Gazebo's default, ODE runs inside Webots, and Drake focuses on model-based design with a detailed hydroelastic contact model. Pick the simulator your task needs, then tune its time step and contact settings.

### Can a game physics engine simulate a robot?

Yes: Bullet, ODE and PhysX serve both games and robotics. The difference is in the setup. Game-style models treat each link as a free body held by joint constraints, which MuJoCo says can cause "inaccuracies and instabilities" in elaborate mechanisms. For robots, use joint coordinates instead: PhysX calls these articulations, and PyBullet uses them by default for URDF robots.
