# Future of Work with Robots

Will robots take our jobs? The honest answer is that economists have been studying this for a decade with good data and still disagree — and the disagreement is more interesting than either confident answer.

## What is not in dispute

Three things are well established and nobody serious argues with them.

**Automation displaces specific jobs.** A machine that palletises boxes removes the job of palletising boxes. This is not controversial; it is the point.

**Automation raises productivity.** Output per worker goes up. That is why firms buy the machines.

**The effect is uneven.** It lands hardest on routine work — tasks that are repetitive, physical and predictable. The OECD's analysis of robot adoption across member countries found robots negatively correlated with employment in elementary occupations and positively correlated with employment of professionals.

## What is in dispute

The argument is about the **net** effect, and it is a real one.

Acemoglu and Restrepo's 2020 study of US local labour markets is the most-cited work here, and it found robust negative effects of robots on both employment and wages across commuting zones — with the impact distinguishable from trade, from other capital investment, and from pre-existing local trends.

Other work points the other way. An ILO study across 35 OECD countries from 1996 to 2020 found that more-robotised economies held onto workers *better* during recessions, with no evidence of jobless recoveries. On that reading automation increases labour-market resilience rather than eroding it.

And the OECD's 2023 Employment Outlook, looking specifically at AI rather than industrial robots, found no sign yet of AI slowing labour demand — while noting that the displacement effect exists and the offsetting productivity effect has not yet shown up at scale.

These are not irreconcilable. They measure different technologies, periods, countries and outcomes. But anyone who tells you the evidence is settled has not read it.

## Where you live changes the answer

This is the part most coverage misses. The ILO's cross-country study found the employment effect of robots small in developed economies but substantially larger in emerging ones over 2005–2014 — and identified a second channel: robots in rich countries reduce offshoring, which removes work from poorer countries that never automated anything.

So a worker's exposure depends less on the technology than on which economy they are standing in, and whether that economy's advantage was cheap labour.

## The three forces, honestly

| Force | What it does | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| Displacement | Machine does the task instead of a person | Palletising, welding, warehouse transport |
| Augmentation | Person does more, or different, work with the machine | [Cobots](/atlas/concept/collaborative-robot) on assembly lines, AI in radiology |
| Creation | Work that did not exist before | Robot integrators, fleet operators, [ROS 2](/atlas/concept/ros2) engineers, safety assessors |

Displacement is fast and visible. Creation is slow and diffuse. That asymmetry is why the public conversation runs ahead of the data — and why the people displaced are rarely the people who get the new jobs, which is a distributional problem rather than a total-employment one.

## Why this matters if you are learning robotics

You are on the creation side of that table. The jobs that have grown are the ones that deploy, integrate, maintain and supervise these systems — and they are growing because the robots are not autonomous enough to run themselves. That is the useful thing to know: the work is in the gap between what a robot can do and what a factory needs doing.

## Where to read the primary evidence

The sources at the bottom of this page are the actual studies, not summaries of them. If you want to form your own view — and on this question you should — start with Acemoglu and Restrepo for the pessimistic case and the ILO's recession study for the optimistic one, and notice how differently they frame the question.
